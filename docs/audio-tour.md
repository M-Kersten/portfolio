# Audio tour scripts

Spoken versions of the voice notes, one per project. Each heading names the
file the recording should get in `src/assets/voice/`. Read them out loud once
before recording and change anything that doesn't sound like you. Each one
runs about a minute and a half at a relaxed pace.

---

## DTT Projects · `dtt-amsterdam.mp3`

Picture the center of Amsterdam. Bikes everywhere, tourists wandering into the
bike lane, and right in the middle of it all, the DTT office. A busy, happening
place. And there I was, standing outside for a job interview, honestly quite
scared to go in.

They gave me a test. I passed it with flying colors. Great! So why did I spend
my first weeks waiting for someone to tap me on the shoulder and say there had
been a mistake? Imposter syndrome. It walked in the door right behind me.

What helped was the people. Colleagues from all over the world, lunch in a mix
of accents, and a few of them became real friends.

My first project was a constellation viewer for Google Cardboard. Yes, the
actual cardboard box you slid your phone into and pressed against your face.
Very old school now. At the time it felt like the future.

Later I got to manage a team. And the honest version is that I didn't meet the
expectations. Managing people, and managing what everyone expects from you, is
a skill of its own, and I was learning it on the job.

It didn't help that the team kept changing. Interns and employees came and
went, so every six months we were retraining half of the team. Like building a
house where half the builders get swapped out twice a year.

Still, we shipped some great apps. My favourite teaches kids to read, together
with their parents or grandparents. Grandma records herself reading a book out
loud, sends it over WhatsApp, the child taps the link, and the app opens with
grandma's voice reading them the story.

## Custom AR framework · `custom-ar-framework.mp3`

While I was at DTT, I had to pick a graduation project. I picked one that
would challenge me. On purpose.

Here was our problem. If you built AR for iPhone, you used Apple's ARKit. For
Android, Google's ARCore. Two frameworks, two ways of doing everything, and we
were juggling both on every single project. So my idea was simple. Build one
framework on top that handles all the interaction, and stop juggling.

Simple idea. Hard job.

A huge amount of time went into the architecture, working out how all the
pieces should fit together. And that's where I really fell in love with this
side of programming. It's one big, complicated puzzle, and for once I had the
time to sit with it and think. I dreamed about it. Literally, at night.

The framework made it into a few projects. One of them was my AR showcase, the
demo we used to show potential clients what AR could do. Its coolest trick was
cross-platform multiplayer anchors. An iPhone and an Android phone, looking at
the same table, seeing the same virtual object in the same spot, in sync. That
felt like magic back then.

It had a short life, though. The framework turned out to be too complicated to
keep going. Apple and Google kept updating their own systems, and every update
meant my layer in between had to catch up again.

## Zwijsen AR books · `zwijsen-ar-books.mp3`

While I was working at DTT, I had some evenings and weekends left over. Then I
got a message. A publishing house called Zwijsen had found my website, and
they had a project for me.

AR books for children.

I couldn't have come up with a cooler project myself.

I love education. If you look at my apps, most of them end up teaching
something. And kids are a wonderful audience to build for. You point a phone at
the page, a character they know starts to move, and their whole face lights
up.

I ended up making seven of these apps, with some editor tooling I built for the
animations. After that came a few listening books.

They were received well. And then came the part nobody puts in their
portfolio: maintenance. Vuforia, the AR technology underneath, deprecated its
licenses. So I had to re-release the apps. That meant App Store review again,
filling in the store pages again, all of it again. It was more work than I
expected. A lot more.

But I handled this whole thing from A to Z, from that first message to the
last store page. I'm still proud of it.

## Popcore games · `popcore-games.mp3`

Around 2020 I got that itch again. The urge to move abroad and find out what
was out there for me. So I went to Berlin, to make casual games.

Casual. That word sounded very relaxed to me. There was nothing casual about
it.

I realised pretty quickly that I was a bit underqualified for the job. So I put
in extra effort, more than my colleagues had to, just to keep up.

I was proud of every game I made. And for every game, the statistics never
quite landed. In that world the numbers decide, so I ended up on the stable
titles, the ones that were already doing well and needed updates and new
features.

After working on more than twenty of these, here's what I see. Making an app
looks easy. A game works because of tiny details you only notice when they're
wrong. How an animation feels under your thumb. The colors that pull your eye
to the right spot. That little moment of anticipation right before the reward.
Controls that simply do what you expect.

Along the way I started teaching new colleagues how to develop with ECS, and I
even got to work on the engine itself a little.

And then I quit. I knew my future wasn't in game development after all. The
passion I'd had for it back in the day was gone. It was the middle of the
pandemic, and I moved back home to look for something else to do.

## ARCam · `arcam.mp3`

My favourite project to date? ARCam. Easy.

For the first time I had real, tangible responsibility. Building the features,
yes, and also setting up the installations myself. I flew across the world with
this kit. Tokyo. San Francisco. Out in the field. Living out of a suitcase.

My coworkers were always enthusiastic, and they let me come up with creative
solutions, which is the best kind of trust you can get. The client was Niantic,
and they were great too. The connections I made, the results we got, it all
felt really good.

And the events were so much fun. People would step up, get their photo with a
Pokémon, and walk away beaming. That never got old.

What it taught me most is that the person standing in front of it is
everything. Getting the interaction right is a delicate balance. You
anticipate what someone needs before they ask. You hide every option they'll
never use. And when something goes wrong, you still show enough that it can be
fixed on the spot.

Now make that work on a device that has to run 24/7. In Tokyo. In San
Francisco. In the middle of a field.

That's a real challenge. I loved every bit of it.

## Lightship Drive · `lightship-drive.mp3`

Getting this one to run. Phew. That was quite something.

It was my first real multiplayer project, the kind that actually had to
deliver. I already knew Niantic's Lightship SDK for AR pretty well by then. The
multiplayer part, I had never touched.

The trick was getting every phone to agree on where the race track sits on the
table, and keeping it there. And then keeping all the players connected.

At the office it went great. We tested it, we raced, everything synced.

Then we got to the show floor.

Major problems. And it wasn't just us scratching our heads. The Niantic
developers who had built that multiplayer feature themselves came over to help
us bugfix, and even they couldn't find what was going on.

The show had to go on. So, the creative solution: if your connection dropped, a
popup appeared. You won! Now hand the phone back.

Everybody's a winner, and the next person gets a turn.

And when it all runs, the end result is really cool. Little AR cars racing
across a real table, with people crowding around to watch.

## Alliander HoloLens suite · `alliander-hololens.mp3`

I joined this team when development was already reaching the end. And during
the intake I said, very confidently: "No worries, I do UX all the time."

Small confession. I had never done UX in a serious way before.

So there I was. Late to the party, with a promise to keep. Hello again,
imposter syndrome. The team made that worse in the best possible way. The
architecture was impressive, everyone was productive and competent. I was
happy to learn quickly, and I had to.

I got familiar with Figma real quick. While I was still finding my way around
the codebase, I was already proposing new designs to the team.

What we built was technically impressive. It just didn't hit the mark on
usability. The HoloLens promises a lot, and in practice it turned out to be
problematic. Think about the whole journey. The kit is lying on a shelf. You
have to get it, set it up, do your viewing, and then share your insights with
everyone else. Every step is a hurdle. Together it was a couple of hurdles too
many for the company to keep investing in it.

My time there was short. I learned a lot, and that's thanks to my coworkers.

## Philips medical XR & AI · `philips-medical-xr.mp3`

Calling this one project isn't really fair. This is everything I've made at
Philips. I've been working there on and off for three years now, and the work
feels meaningful. And exciting.

The department is called the Immersive Lab. That name already tells you
something about the creative and technical freedom I got there.

For Lumiguide I built a catheter simulation. You hold a wire, you guide it, and
on a monitor you see it move, just like you'd expect to see it in the operating
room.

Then there were experiments with virtual avatars. Faces that move their mouths
along with the words, using facial animation visemes, with AI behind them so
you can actually have a conversation.

And then a real study: simulating a heart rate in a MetaHuman face, so AI
models could be trained on it. That was a tricky one. I wasn't there for the
whole project, but I was glad to help and get some experience with Unreal.

It also made me very happy that I chose Unity.

## Amsterdam AI guides · `amsterdam-ai.mp3`

At Philips, working with AI avatars had been a success. So at Rebels we wanted
to try something with them too. I had some spare hours, and I proposed a
prototype. You could meet people in Amsterdam and hear their story. From them.
Sort of.

This was back when every new AI capability felt like a little fireworks show,
and I wanted to cram as many of them into one project as I could.

The project itself failed. I'll just say that plainly.

What I got out of it was doing a project in a truly professional way, start to
finish. Proposal, planning, architecture, UX, deliveries. And then the whole
outside world part too: marketing, tutorials, blog posts. That experience has
been really useful. It also got a lot of views, which is nice.

And it made me more discerning about AI. Where its boundaries are. One of them
is very clear to me now. Human connection. You can't replace it.

## Virtuele Brigade · `virtuele-brigade.mp3`

In terms of technical complexity, this project has become the highlight of my
career. Whether that's something to boast about, I'm still not sure.

When I joined, the project was already years old, and my job was to add
multiplayer. Bolting multiplayer onto a project at that stage is like changing
the engine of a car while it's driving down the road. The lessons have to keep
going. And the boss expects it to be like flipping a switch. Single player,
click, multiplayer. Done.

The reality was way more difficult.

Working for defense was never something I expected to do. I'm a nerd! Now that
I've been there, I see it as a valuable look into a world I didn't know at all.

You see how tradition, rank and politics seep into the technical work you're
trying to do. That can leave you cynical. I never let it get to me, and neither
did my team at the KMar. We look at what defense needs and how we can help. And
it's education again, which I love.

With all the money flowing into defense now, there's room to innovate again.
The hardware I get to test things on is seriously motivating me to try new
things.

And during my time there, AI really got going. I watched my own work change.
I became much more productive. It also gave me doubts about the future.
